# Accessible Meetings Checklist

The pre-flight list we use at the American Society for Deaf Children before a meeting, class, webinar, or conference session goes live. It assumes a mixed audience: Deaf and hard-of-hearing people, people with low vision or blindness, people using screen readers, people whose first language is ASL or Spanish, and people joining from a phone in a car park.

The rule behind every item: nobody should have to ask for access after the event has started. If they have to ask, we were late.

Copy it, edit it, argue with it. Pull requests welcome.

## Two weeks out

- [ ] Book interpreters (ASL, and Spanish or other spoken-language interpreters as needed). For sessions over an hour, book two ASL interpreters so they can rotate.
- [ ] Book captioning. Human CART captioning is the standard for anything public-facing or recorded. Automatic captions are a fallback for small internal meetings, and someone corrects the transcript afterward.
- [ ] Decide whether a separate low-vision or visually impaired room runs alongside the main session (large text, verbal description of every visual, slower pacing). At ASDC every class has one.
- [ ] Put the accessibility arrangements in the invitation itself: "ASL interpreters and live captioning provided. Tell us by [date] if you need anything else." Include a contact person, not a generic inbox.
- [ ] Ask registrants what they need on the registration form, with a free-text box, and read the answers.

## One week out

- [ ] Send slides, handouts, and any video to interpreters and captioners in advance, with names, acronyms, and technical terms spelled out.
- [ ] Make every document accessible before it is shared: real headings (Heading 1, Heading 2) rather than bold text pretending to be headings; alt text on every image that carries meaning and empty alt on decorative ones; PDFs exported as tagged PDFs with a checked reading order, with the source document shared alongside; tables with header rows marked and no merged cells; links that say where they go ("Register for the November class"), never "click here".
- [ ] Test the email announcement with a screen reader (NVDA, JAWS, or VoiceOver) before it goes out. Check that the reading order makes sense, images have alt text, and the call to action is a real link.
- [ ] Check color contrast on slides (4.5:1 for normal text, 3:1 for large text). Do not use color alone to carry meaning.
- [ ] Confirm the video platform settings: captions enabled, multi-pinning or spotlight available for interpreters, chat accessible, screen share will not hide the interpreter.

## Day of

- [ ] Join 15 minutes early with interpreters and captioners. Check audio, check that interpreters are pinned, check captions are flowing.
- [ ] Assign one person to monitor chat, captions, and raised hands for access problems. That person is not the presenter.
- [ ] Open with the access arrangements out loud and in the chat: where captions are, how to pin the interpreter, who to message if something breaks.
- [ ] Presenters describe what is on screen. "This chart shows enrollment going from 5 to 1,200 between June and August" rather than "as you can see here".
- [ ] One person speaks at a time. Say your name before you speak in larger meetings.
- [ ] Give interpreters and captioners a break every 20 to 30 minutes for anything over an hour.
- [ ] If recording, say so, and say that captions and a transcript will be shared.

## After

- [ ] Share the corrected transcript, the captioned recording, and the accessible versions of all materials within a set number of days. Put the number in your policy and keep it.
- [ ] Ask what did not work. Keep a running log of access failures by type (captions dropped, interpreter not pinned, PDF untagged) and fix the pattern, not just the instance.
- [ ] Pay interpreters and captioners on time. This is an accessibility item.

## Why some of these are on the list

Most access failures we have seen were not malice or ignorance. They were a presenter who said "as you can see," a PDF exported with the wrong settings, or an interpreter who was booked but never pinned. The checklist exists because good intentions do not survive a busy Tuesday.
